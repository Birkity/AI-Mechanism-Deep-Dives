I understand now that an evaluation number is only meaningful when its denominator is honest and its chance baseline is made explicit. For abstention-prone evaluators, answered-case accuracy is conditional on the model deciding to answer, so coverage must be reported and abstentions must be checked by slice. For Amir's question, the complete answer requires three metrics: Cohen's kappa (chance corrected from the product of actual marginal rates), PABAK (chance fixed at 0.5 to remove prevalence sensitivity), and Gwet's AC1 (chance estimated from average proportions rather than the product of marginals). These produce kappa 0.46, PABAK 0.83, and AC1 0.90 on the same data — not because the data changed but because each models a different notion of chance. The formula 2Po − 1 for PABAK is not arbitrary: it is kappa under the assumption that the true label base rate is 50/50, and subtracting 0.5 removes the baseline a random classifier achieves under balanced conditions. AC1 is more stable than kappa under high prevalence because its expected-chance term (Pe ≈ 0.156) is driven by the average proportion across passes rather than the product of skewed marginals, which prevents Pe from inflating toward 1 as labels pile up in one class.

The most important structural lesson is the methodology threshold problem: requiring >80% raw agreement when chance agreement is 84.4% is a threshold below chance. Any rubric passes it regardless of quality. The threshold should be converted to κ ≥ 0.4 per dimension or set above the chance floor in raw agreement terms. The `workflow_correctness` dimension at estimated κ ≈ 0.3 falls in the "fair" band and should not be used as the sole training signal until its rubric is rebuilt with explicit decision examples for multi-step edge cases.
